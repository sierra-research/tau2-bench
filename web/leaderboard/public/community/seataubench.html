SEATauBench: Progressively Adapting Tool-Agent-User Evaluation Into Low-Resource Southeast Asian Languages

October 2026 · Findings of EMNLP 2026

My Chiffon Nguyen†, Aulia Adila†, Saksorn Ruangtanusak†, Kittiphat Leesombatwathana†, Vissuta Gunawan Lim†, Patomporn Payoungkhamdee & Samuel Cahyawijaya

SEACrowd · AI Singapore · SCB DataX, SCBX Group · Chulalongkorn University · VISTEC · University of Indonesia · Cohere

† The first five authors contributed equally.

TL;DR

SEATauBench adapts τ²-bench to Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Mandarin Chinese, and Filipino across Airline, Retail, and Telecom. Four settings separate dialogue, tool understanding, and domain reasoning while keeping the underlying tasks and scoring aligned.

Multilingual language models can hold a conversation, but can they complete a customer-service task when the tools, policies, and data also change language? Agent capabilities in Southeast Asian languages remain poorly understood, and English-only evaluation gives an incomplete picture.

Three ideas SeaTauBench brings together

  1. Aligned tasks across languages. Evaluate the same customer-service tasks in Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Mandarin Chinese, and Filipino, with English as the reference.
  2. Separate the language surfaces. Change dialogue, tool specifications, or the full domain context to examine where performance changes.
  3. Measure success and consistency. Report task success alongside performance across repeated trials, so a single successful run does not tell the whole story.

Four settings isolate different language surfaces. S1 is the English reference; S2 localizes dialogue, S3 tool schemas, and S4 the full environment. Each row is a separate condition.

How we create SeaTauBench

The adaptation pipeline separates what the agent sees from what the benchmark executes. Offline translation adapts the language while preserving identifiers and executable structure; runtime mappings connect localized tool arguments and responses to canonical values. Automated checks and native-speaker review then assess preservation and translation quality, with approved corrections recorded in a separate reviewed release.

01 / TRANSLATE

Protect what must stay exact

A glossary guides offline translation. Identifiers, executable tokens, and structure are preserved across task descriptions, policies, schemas, and databases.

02 / EXECUTE

Keep actions aligned

Runtime mappings normalize localized tool arguments for execution and localize returned content. Scoring compares canonical outcomes.

03 / AUDIT

Review language and behavior

Automated preservation checks and native-speaker review assess translation quality. Approved corrections are recorded in a separate reviewed release.

Localization must preserve the task the agent is asked to complete.

Results

Task success falls as the environment is localized

Changing the conversation language alone preserves much of the English performance, while full localization exposes a larger task-success gap. The five panels below compare Thai, Vietnamese, Indonesian, Chinese, and Filipino across S1 (English baseline), S2 (L2 interaction), S3 (L2 tools), and S4 (L2 domain). Blue circles show mean task success (pass@1), and red squares show repeated-run robustness (ρ³); the connected series summarize each setting, while the individual points show model averages.

Task success and robustness reveal different aspects of multilingual performance. In the plot below, the radar charts compare pass@1 and ρ³ across English and the five target languages, while the domain panels compare English and non-English means for Airline, Retail, and Telecom. pass@1 measures mean task success; ρ³ relates success across three trials to pass@1, so higher robustness does not by itself mean that more tasks are solved. The gaps vary by model, language, and domain, showing why English results alone give an incomplete picture.

Robustness across repeated trials

q is the number of trials. Robustness compares success in all q trials with mean single-trial success; this benchmark reports ρ³ (q = 3).

ρq = passq pass@1 \rho^{q} = \frac{\mathrm{pass}^{q}}{\mathrm{pass}@1}

Metrics, source, and methodology notes

Success across repeated trialspass@1 is mean task success. pass² and pass³ measure success across two and three trials, respectively; they are not success on any one attempt.

Consistency is a different questionρ³ = pass³ / pass@1 for each published cell. We average reported ρ³ values separately. High robustness can coexist with low overall task success.

Source: paper v2, Tables 17–19 and 21. Equal weight per language/domain cell, not per individual task. Values retain the paper’s precision; ties at the displayed precision share a rank. Qwen3-235B denotes Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507.

Scores describe the frozen experiment snapshot, not the later reviewed translation release. S3’s appendix lists three models although the main-text aggregate comparison describes two. Rankings here are computed from the appendix cells and may differ from rounded narrative summaries. The benchmark uses 50 Airline tasks and 114 tasks each in Retail and Telecom, with three trials per task. The user simulator is Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct; natural-language assertions use GPT-4.1.
